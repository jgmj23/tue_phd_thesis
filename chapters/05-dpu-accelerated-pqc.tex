\chapter{DPU-Accelerated Post-Quantum Cryptography}
\label{ch:dpu-pqc}

PQC is the computationally secure counterpart to the quantum key distribution of Part~\ref{part:qkd}. Where QKD grounds its security in physics, PQC keeps the familiar shape of classical public-key cryptography but swaps the underlying mathematics for problems believed to resist quantum as well as classical attack. This chapter builds from those mathematical foundations up to the practical question that motivates the two contributions it contains: once the algorithms are fixed by standardization, \emph{where} in a datacenter should they actually run, and on which processor?

\section{Foundations of Public-Key Cryptography}
\label{sec:pqc-foundations}

The fundamental goal of public-key cryptography is to identify highly asymmetric mathematical problems. To be viable for securing the internet, an underlying mathematical problem must satisfy three core properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetry (Trapdoor Function):} It must be nearly impossible for an untrusted attacker to solve, yet trivial to compute for trusted parties who possess a secret key.
    \item \textbf{Efficiency:} The operations must be computationally lightweight enough for standard computers, edge devices, and network equipment to execute without introducing unacceptable latency.
    \item \textbf{Exponential Scaling:} The difficulty of the problem must scale exponentially with the parameter size, ensuring that brute-force attacks remain forever out of reach.
\end{enumerate}

The cryptography that secures today's internet rests on two number-theoretic problems that beautifully satisfy these requirements: factoring large integers (RSA) and computing discrete logarithms (Diffie--Hellman, elliptic-curve cryptography). However, as established in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, Shor's algorithm dismantles both. Because these classical problems rely on underlying periodic algebraic structures, Shor's algorithm can solve them in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer.

The response is not to abandon public-key cryptography but to rebuild it on different foundations. The post-quantum transition requires primitives that lack this exploitable periodic structure, ensuring they remain hard even when the attacker is equipped with a cryptographically relevant quantum computer.

\section{Cryptographic Primitives: KEMs and Signatures}
\label{sec:pqc-primitives}

Public-key cryptography performs two functional jobs, and the standardized post-quantum algorithms fall into the corresponding two categories. Key-encapsulation mechanisms (KEMs) establish a shared symmetric secret between two parties over an insecure channel---the post-quantum replacement for RSA key transport and Diffie--Hellman exchange. Digital signatures provide authentication and non-repudiation.

Because the exact mathematical implementation depends entirely on the chosen algorithm family (e.g., lattice vs.\ code-based), a KEM is best abstracted as three functional operations (Figure~\ref{fig:pqc-primitives}(a)). \emph{Key generation} $\mathsf{KeyGen}$ produces a public/secret key pair $(\mathit{pk}, \mathit{sk})$. \emph{Encapsulation} $\mathsf{Encaps}$, run by the initiator against the recipient's public key, outputs a ciphertext $\mathit{ct}$ and a freshly derived shared secret $\mathit{ss}$. \emph{Decapsulation} $\mathsf{Decaps}$, run by the recipient with the secret key, recovers that same shared secret from the ciphertext. This shared secret is then used to key a fast symmetric cipher (such as AES) that encrypts the actual bulk message traffic. A signature scheme follows a similar three-part functional structure (Figure~\ref{fig:pqc-primitives}(b)): $\mathsf{KeyGen}$ produces a keypair, $\mathsf{Sign}$ maps a message and the secret key to a signature $\sigma$, and $\mathsf{Verify}$ tests a message--signature pair against the public key. Rather than establishing a secret, it certifies a message's origin. In deployed systems, the public keys used for this verification are typically bound to an identity and distributed by a certificate authority (CA), ensuring that a client can trust the signature on a downloaded file or a server's TLS certificate.

\section{The NIST Standardization Process}
\label{sec:pqc-standards}

Confidence in a new cryptographic primitive is not established by a security proof alone; it is earned through years of concentrated cryptanalysis. To orchestrate that scrutiny, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) launched an open, multi-round post-quantum standardization process in 2016, inviting submissions from the global research community and winnowing them over successive rounds of public analysis. The process culminated in 2024 with the first three finalized standards: FIPS~203 (ML-KEM, a lattice key-encapsulation mechanism derived from Kyber), FIPS~204 (ML-DSA, a lattice signature derived from Dilithium), and FIPS~205 (the stateless hash-based signature SLH-DSA)~\cite{nistfips203,nistfips204,nistfips205}.

The prominence of lattices among these standards created a strategic concern: an over-reliance on a single mathematical family. The risk is not hypothetical. During the standardization process, the isogeny-based candidate SIKE---a scheme that had survived several rounds and was widely considered a strong contender---was completely broken. In a stark reminder of the fragility of new cryptography, the attack did not require a quantum computer or even a datacenter; researchers dismantled it in a matter of hours using a single core of a standard desktop PC, applying a mathematical theorem from 1997. To hedge against a comparable future break of lattices, NIST continued a dedicated fourth round for key-encapsulation mechanisms drawn from other families, and in 2025 selected the code-based HQC as a backup standard~\cite{nistir8545}.

This deliberate diversity motivates the scope of Part~\ref{part:pqc}. Rather than treating post-quantum cryptography as a single monolithic workload, this thesis studies representatives of both leading families: the lattice-based ML-KEM and ML-DSA, whose polynomial arithmetic dominates the near-term deployment landscape, and the code-based HQC, whose contrasting computational structure both stresses hardware differently and represents the community's insurance policy against a lattice failure.

\section{Mathematical Families of Post-Quantum Cryptography}
\label{sec:pqc-families}

Three mathematical families underpin the standardized algorithms and the candidates studied in this thesis. While a full mathematical formulation of these schemes is out of scope for this text, the following subsections provide an intuition for the underlying structures that guarantee their security and dictate their computational profiles.

\subsection{Lattice-Based Cryptography}
\label{subsec:lattice-crypto}

A lattice is the set of all integer linear combinations of a set of basis vectors---an infinite, regularly spaced grid of points in $n$-dimensional space. Its security rests on problems that are easy to state but hard to solve in high dimension. The shortest-vector problem (SVP) asks for the shortest nonzero point in the lattice; given a ``bad'' (nearly parallel) basis, even approximating it becomes intractable as the dimension grows. 

The learning-with-errors (LWE) problem, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lattice-intuition}, is its cryptographically convenient cousin: recover a secret vector from many noisy linear equations, where a small random error hides the exact solution.

To make this concrete, consider a toy instance of LWE. Without errors, recovering a secret vector $\mathbf{s}$ from a set of observations $A\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{b}$ is trivial using standard Gaussian elimination---the algebraic method of isolating variables through successive addition and subtraction of equations. 

For example, given two exact equations modulo 17:
\begin{align}
    s_1 + 2s_2 &= 7 \pmod{17} \\
    3s_1 + 2s_2 &= 13 \pmod{17}
\end{align}
Subtracting the first from the second immediately isolates $2s_1 = 6$, revealing $s_1 = 3$. 

LWE perturbs each equation slightly by adding a small, secret error vector $\mathbf{e}$:
\begin{equation}
    A\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} \pmod q
\end{equation}
If an attacker attempts to apply the same subtraction to noisy equations, the math breaks down. Because each original equation contained an unknown error of, say, $\pm 1$, the combined equation now has an error of up to $\pm 2$. In a realistic cryptographic setting with hundreds of variables, isolating a single variable requires adding and subtracting hundreds of equations. The errors rapidly accumulate, expanding beyond the modulo boundary $q$ and completely washing out the secret signal in mathematical noise. What was a trivial algebra problem becomes exponentially hard.

The structured, efficient variant used by the standardized algorithms---the key-encapsulation mechanism ML-KEM and the digital signature ML-DSA---is the module learning-with-errors (MLWE) problem. MLWE replaces these scalar integers with polynomials, balancing the strong security of unstructured LWE against the compact keys and fast arithmetic that a deployable scheme requires. Crucially, the best known quantum algorithms for these lattice problems offer only modest polynomial speedups, not the exponential collapse that Shor inflicts on factoring.

\subsection{Code-Based Cryptography}
\label{subsec:code-crypto}

The second family draws its hardness from coding theory. Decoding a general (random) linear error-correcting code---recovering the original message from a received word corrupted by errors, given only the code's parity-check structure---is an NP-hard problem known as syndrome decoding. In computational complexity theory, an NP-hard classification indicates that no known algorithm can efficiently solve arbitrary instances of the problem; brute-force search remains the only general approach, an effort that scales exponentially as the code size grows.

To visualize this, consider a toy instance using binary arithmetic (often denoted $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, where addition is simply the logical XOR operation, $1 \oplus 1 = 0$).

A parity-check code works by enforcing rules on a message. For example, a rule might state that bits 1, 3, and 4 of a valid message must always sum to 0 (meaning they contain an even number of 1s). If a received message violates this rule---summing to 1 instead---it produces a \emph{syndrome}, a mathematical symptom indicating that an error has occurred.

In code-based cryptography, the public key is a massive matrix $H$ defining thousands of these overlapping, seemingly random parity-check rules. When an encrypting party wants to send a secure message, they intentionally introduce an error vector $\mathbf{e}$ containing a small, specific number of flipped bits (referred to as its \emph{Hamming weight}). They compute the syndrome:
\begin{equation}
    H\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{s} \pmod 2
\end{equation}
The attacker intercepts the syndrome $\mathbf{s}$ and knows the rules $H$, and must find the specific sparse error vector $\mathbf{e}$ that triggered those exact rule violations. While computing the syndrome in the forward direction is a fast sequence of bitwise XOR operations, working backwards to find which few bits were flipped out of thousands is akin to solving a massive, unstructured logic puzzle. It is computationally intractable without a cryptographic trapdoor.

Code-based cryptosystems exploit this asymmetry, deriving their security from a mathematical foundation completely unrelated to lattices. In these schemes, the public key describes a code that looks random to an attacker but carries a hidden structure the legitimate holder can decode efficiently. The standardized algorithm representing this family is HQC, a key-encapsulation mechanism designed as an independent hedge against a potential break of lattices. While HQC uses structured quasi-cyclic codes to maintain manageable parameters, its public keys and ciphertexts remain significantly larger than those of lattice schemes.

\subsection{Hash-Based Cryptography}
\label{subsec:hash-crypto}

The third family derives its security entirely from the well-understood properties of cryptographic hash functions. It represents a fundamentally different style of cryptography compared to lattices or codes: rather than introducing any new algebraic hardness assumptions, hash-based cryptography relies solely on the inability to invert a standard hash function (preimage resistance) or find two inputs that produce the same output (collision resistance).

Because this mathematical one-way street naturally maps to proving knowledge of a secret without revealing it, this family is inherently suited to building digital signatures rather than key-encapsulation mechanisms. The standardization process selected SLH-DSA as the representative for this family. SLH-DSA constructs its signatures by chaining together thousands of individual hash evaluations, organizing them into a massive, stateless verification structure that allows the same public key to be used repeatedly without keeping track of past signatures.

This architectural reliance on standard symmetric hash functions makes SLH-DSA exceptionally conservative---if the underlying hash function survives the quantum era, the signature scheme survives with it.

None of these three families exposes the periodic algebraic structure that Shor's algorithm exploits, which is precisely why they are all considered quantum-resistant.

\section{Mapping Algorithms to Architecture}
\label{sec:pqc-offload}

The migration to post-quantum cryptography is not finished when a standard is published. The fundamentally different mathematical structures of these algorithm families---polynomial rings, huge binary matrices, or deep hash trees---lead to profoundly different computational profiles. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:pqc-comparison}, lattice schemes (ML-KEM, ML-DSA) spend most of their time in polynomial arithmetic that vectorizes well on single instruction, multiple data (SIMD) units, though the rejection sampling in ML-DSA introduces variable-time execution that complicates batching. Code-based HQC is dominated by the movement of its massive keys and ciphertexts through memory, making it fundamentally memory-bound. Hash-based schemes like SLH-DSA are bottlenecked almost entirely by raw symmetric hashing throughput.

An algorithm that executes efficiently in a single-threaded microbenchmark may behave disastrously under the provisioning constraints of a production datacenter. Two pressures dominate. First, the inflated size of post-quantum public keys, ciphertexts, and signatures can trigger IP fragmentation in TLS or IPsec handshakes, raising latency and packet-loss probability. Second, because datacenters terminate thousands of connections per second, cryptographic operations must be batched deeply to be viable. A memory-bound workload and a compute-bound, SIMD-friendly one respond very differently to the pressure of concurrent traffic and batching.

That observation turns algorithm selection into an architecture problem: on which processor should the cryptographic work run, how should requests be batched, how much latency can the service tolerate, and which memory buses become the bottleneck? The DPU substrate introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} opens a design space with three placement options, each with a distinct cost structure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Host x86 CPU.} The conventional choice, with abundant single-thread performance---but every cycle spent on cryptography is a cycle taken from the revenue-generating workloads the datacenter exists to run, and contention with those workloads makes latency unpredictable.
    \item \textbf{DPU Arm cores.} Executing on the DPU's general-purpose Arm cores isolates the cryptographic work from the host entirely. Throughput per core is modest, but the isolation buys predictability: the crypto runs regardless of what the host is doing, and vice versa.
    \item \textbf{Embedded GPU.} A GPU integrated onto the DPU brings massive parallelism to bear on batched operations. On integrated designs the Arm cores and GPU share a single memory domain, so input data need not cross the PCIe bus---the very transfer that often dominates the cost of offloading to a discrete accelerator.
\end{itemize}

The distinction between crossing the PCIe boundary and sharing one memory domain is decisive for batched cryptography, where the data-movement cost can eclipse the arithmetic itself.

\section{Contribution and Articles}
\label{sec:pqc-context}

This chapter is built around two contributions that study how post-quantum cryptographic workloads can be executed efficiently on heterogeneous, programmable network hardware, jointly addressing \textbf{RQ1}.

The first contribution (P4), \enquote{Offloading Post-Quantum Cryptography to Data Processing Units: A Performance Analysis of HQC Key Encapsulation}, presents the first experimental evaluation of offloading the code-based HQC key-encapsulation mechanism to a high-capacity DPU. It compares host x86 execution against execution on the Arm cores of an NVIDIA BlueField-3 across three security levels and four server-load regimes, in both single- and multi-threaded configurations. This work was published in \emph{IEEE Access}. Its central finding is that architectural isolation, rather than raw speed, is the decisive advantage: while the host is faster on an idle machine, the DPU delivers both lower latency under contention and near-deterministic timing regardless of host load.

The second contribution (P5), \enquote{GPU-Accelerated Post-Quantum Cryptography on Embedded DPU Architecture}, develops and characterizes a heterogeneous DPU architecture that places GPU compute directly in the network datapath. Exploiting an embedded GPU that shares a single memory domain with the DPU's Arm cores---eliminating the PCIe transfer that penalizes discrete accelerators---it evaluates the lattice schemes ML-KEM and ML-DSA across security levels and batch sizes. This manuscript is under review at \emph{IEEE Networking Letters}. It identifies the batch size at which GPU acceleration overtakes CPU execution and quantifies the resulting expansion in secure-connection capacity.

Beyond these articles, the work on post-quantum cryptographic offload also contributed to a filed patent application (I2), \enquote{Computational Offloading of Operations Necessary for Post-Quantum Cryptography on Network Interface Cards}.

Read together, the two contributions map the offload design space of Section~\ref{sec:pqc-offload} onto measured hardware. They show that the right placement depends on the algorithm's computational profile and the deployment's dominant constraint: the memory-bound HQC benefits most from the DPU's isolation and determinism under contention, whereas the SIMD-friendly lattice schemes benefit most from the parallelism of an embedded GPU once enough requests can be batched together, with the shared memory domain removing the data-movement penalty that would otherwise erode the gain. In both cases the lever that makes post-quantum cryptography practical at datacenter scale is not the algorithm alone but its co-design with the processor and memory hierarchy it runs on. Both studies, however, characterize the primitive in isolation; the next chapter carries it into a complete edge-AI pipeline, where the offload must hold up amid competing data ingest, preprocessing, and inference.
