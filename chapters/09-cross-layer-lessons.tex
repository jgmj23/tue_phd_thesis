\section{Cross-Layer Lessons}

The three layers developed across this thesis are not independent. Their dependencies
form strict, largely unidirectional chains that, taken together, answer RQ4 and dictate
system design:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Device to Protocol:} Source-level spectral purity and FSR stability
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:quantum-sources}) dictate the baseline error rates. These error rates
    determine the size of the LDPC matrices required in QKD post-processing
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:qkd-dpu}).
    \item \textbf{Protocol to Infrastructure:} The matrix dimensions and the determinism of
    PQC rejection sampling (Chapter~\ref{ch:dpu-pqc}) dictate compute latency. This latency
    determines whether the algorithm can be placed inline on a DPU without starving the
    line-rate datapath (Chapters~\ref{ch:dpu-pqc} and~\ref{ch:edge-ai}).
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure to Protocol:} The ordering, timing, and visibility properties
    of the RDMA fabric (Chapter~\ref{ch:rdma}) determine whether the mathematical protections of
    the cryptography actually survive deployment against a network-level adversary, and what
    signal remains for a defender once payloads are opaque (Chapter~\ref{ch:confidential-ai}).
\end{itemize}

This cross-layer perspective is the reason the dissertation combines topics---photonics,
cryptography, and datacenter networking---that would otherwise appear separate. The unifying
question that runs through every chapter is how secure communication systems can be made
scalable and deployable when the binding constraints are distributed across physical,
mathematical, and operational boundaries.
